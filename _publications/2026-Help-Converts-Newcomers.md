excerpt: "Whether people who receive help online go on to help others is an open question. Prior observational studies report mixed results, and designs that ignore prior user activity may overstate reciprocity. We develop a matched difference-in-differences survival analysis of over 21 million Stack Overflow questions, comparing users whose questions were answered with propensity-score-matched users whose questions were not. We don't ask broadly whether reciprocity works in general, but locate the narrow conditions under which it effects user behavior. Users whose questions were answered helped others slightly more often (increment at answer arrival, hazard ratio [HR] = 1.049, 95% CI [1.044, 1.054]). The response depended on tenure: new users responded most (HR = 1.071 in the first week, 95% CI [1.056, 1.086]), while the longest-tenured users did not respond measurably. The tenure gradient is the most robust pattern we find. Timing mattered too, though far more fragilely: users answered within 30 to 60 min responded most strongly, while answers under 30 min show no elevation. Most of our data predate 2020, and the generalized reciprocity effect among new users does not survive a split at ChatGPT's release, so these estimates reflect a pre-generative-AI baseline. We review gratitude, moral obligation, social expectation, and incentive displacement as possible psychological mechanisms but cannot measure them. On Stack Overflow, generalized reciprocity is less a general driver of cooperation than a recruitment channel for contributions from new users: designers gain most by getting newcomers' questions answered."
