<section>
  <h2 class="fx__title">Structure and culture as sources of anticipation</h2>
  <div class="fx__prose">
    <p>The standard prescription for coordinating a culturally diverse workforce is structural: make roles predictable, couple them tightly, and people can anticipate each other from the role system rather than from a shared background. That assumes structure and culture are interchangeable. They are not. Role systems generate <em>scripted</em> anticipation, which carries routine exchange; a shared cultural background generates <em>communicative</em> anticipation — the common ground partners need to renegotiate coordination in real time.</p>

    <p>Across roughly five million directed dyad-match observations from professional European soccer, a shared official language predicts higher pass rates between teammates, and the advantage concentrates precisely where structure offers least support. For flexibility the pattern reverses: tight role coupling amplifies the cultural advantage rather than substituting for it. Organizations that pair diverse workforces with rigid structures run efficiently on routine work and adapt badly.</p>

    <p><a href="https://osf.io/preprints/socarxiv/wpy8g_v2" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Substitutes or Complements? Role Systems and Culture as Sources of Predictive Knowledge for Coordination</a> — SocArXiv preprint.</p>
  </div>
</section>

<section>
  <h2 class="fx__title">What autonomy does to who does what</h2>
  <div class="fx__prose">
    <p>Hand out task autonomy without a shared, global view of the work and coordination does not become freer — it reorganizes around whoever already had context. Using LLM classification over 240,000+ real task assignments, this line traces how self-assignment legitimates a quiet resegregation of work by gender, and what the design remedy looks like. Winner of the OMT Best Student Paper Award and a Best Paper designation at the Academy of Management, and a finalist for the Edgar Schein Best Student Paper Award.</p>

    <p>Working paper: <em>The Agency Gap in Task Assignments: Gender, Legitimation, and the Unintended Consequences of Task Autonomy.</em></p>
  </div>
</section>

<section>
  <h2 class="fx__title">What sustains contribution on platforms</h2>
  <div class="fx__prose">
    <p>Earlier work on how online platforms produce engagement and social connection. A lab experiment on reputation systems finds that publicizing past behavior does not, as feared, erode cohesive ties — market participants form relationships as an emotional byproduct of exchange rather than for purely functional reasons. A companion field study on 21 million Stack Overflow questions shows generalized reciprocity works as a <em>recruitment</em> mechanism: receiving help makes newcomers contribute, and the effect fades as platform-specific status incentives take over.</p>

    <p><a href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/01902725241289880" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">How Do Reputation Systems Affect Commitment and Social Cohesion in Economic Exchange?</a> — <em>Social Psychology Quarterly</em>. And <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.03209" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Help Converts Newcomers, Not Veterans</a> — arXiv preprint, R&amp;R at <em>Computers in Human Behavior</em>.</p>
  </div>
